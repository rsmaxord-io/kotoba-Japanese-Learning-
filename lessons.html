The route

Ten units. One long conversation.

Each unit has ten levels. The early units build the mouth and the survival kit; the middle turns phrases into grammar; the last four units stretch you into nuance, opinion and long-form Japanese. You do not need to finish every level before speaking to someone — but the order is designed so that each level only asks for what the previous one gave you.

The study-guide spine

Every level opens with a small guide bar naming its textbook reference — for example Genki I · L4 · Minna 11. The order is ours, but the grammar sequence follows the standard teaching order used by Genki I & II, Minna no Nihongo, Tobira and Shin Kanzen Master. How long does 100 levels take?

At 20–30 focused minutes a day, most learners move through N5 in 2–3 months, N4 by month 7, N3 by month 14, and N2/N1 material over the following year. That is a guide, not a race. One level a day is excellent. One level a week is still progress.

What “N1” means here

N1 is not a finish line where the language stops. It means you can follow a newspaper column, a workplace meeting, a joke with a double meaning, and a dialect joke without needing the English. Levels 86–100 aim at that kind of range: dense reading, polite negotiation, casual banter and the confidence to ask a good question.

Every level, from 1

Your turn: write it, then say it.

Recognition is not production. Every level ends with one short writing task and one speaking task, the same three-step scaffold each time, success criteria you can check yourself against, and a model answer in standard Japanese and in Kansai-ben. You do not have to feel ready. Producing the sentence is what teaches it.

Writing · 3 steps

Steal the frame, change the content

  1. Steal the frame. Copy the shape of one example in the level. Keep its ending.
  2. Change only the content. Your city, your day, your opinion. No new grammar.
  3. Read it back once. Say it out loud before you open the model.
Show a model

きょうは しごとの あとで、えきの ちかくで ラーメンを たべました。あめが ふっていたので、かさを かりました。うちへ かえって、すこし にほんごを べんきょうします。

Today, after work, I ate ramen near the station. Because it was raining, I borrowed an umbrella. I will go home and study Japanese for a while.

Speaking · 3 steps

Thirty seconds, three times

  1. Plan in Japanese. Three beats: what happened, one detail, how you felt.
  2. Say it three times. Accuracy first, rhythm second, nerve third.
  3. Record once and listen. For long vowels and small っ — not for a perfect accent.
Show a speaking drill

Prompt: きのう、なにを しましたか。 Answer in three sentences, then answer again in Kansai-ben with 〜たで / 〜てん. Do not write it down first.

Why it works

Output forces grammar

Understanding a sentence and building one use different work. When you have to say it, you notice the particle you skipped and the ending you guessed. That gap is the lesson. This is the output hypothesis: production makes you process grammar more deeply than recognition does.

How to use the model

Compare, do not copy

Write first, then open the model. Ask three questions: what did they do that I did not, what did I do better for my own life, and which one sentence would I actually say? Change one thing, say it again, and move on. A finished level is worth more than a perfect one.

The twenty-minute loop

One session, three jobs. The order matters more than the length.

  1. 05Review

    Open the review dock. Re-say the lines that are due, from memory, before you look.

  2. 10New level

    Read the pattern, the dialogue and the examples. Say every line out loud once.

  3. 05Output

    Write the task, say it three times, then compare with the model.

Learning science

Six ideas to help your learning stick.

Nothing here is a trick. These are the effects that keep showing up when researchers test how people actually retain a language, and each one is visible in how the levels are arranged.

01

Retrieval practice

Roediger & Karpicke, 2006

Pulling something out of memory strengthens it more than putting it in again. Try recalling a phrase before you reread it.

Here: the model answer is hidden until after you have produced your own sentence.

02

Spaced repetition

Ebbinghaus, 1885 · Cepeda et al., 2006

Memory decays on a curve, and each successful recall flattens it. Reviewing just before you would forget is the efficient moment.

Here: every completed level returns at 1, 3, 7 and 21 days in the review dock.

03

Desirable difficulty

Bjork & Bjork, 2011

Conditions that make practice feel harder often make learning last longer. Fluency during practice is a poor signal of what you know.

Here: no English on the first pass, speaking without a script, and a model you meet only after trying.

04

Comprehensible input, one step up

Krashen, 1982

You acquire most from material that is almost understandable — one unknown item in a familiar frame, not ten.

Here: exactly one new pattern per level, with the rest recycled from earlier levels.

05

Output and noticing

Swain, 1985 · Schmidt, 1990

Speaking and writing push you to notice the gap between what you meant and what you could produce. That noticing drives the next bit of grammar.

Here: a writing and speaking task at every level, with criteria and a model to compare against.

06

Interleaving

Rohrer & Taylor, 2007

Mixing old and new material beats blocking one topic for hours, because you have to choose the right tool each time.

Here: the review dock mixes levels from different units, and the “due” filter builds a mixed session for you.

One more thing the research is clear about: streaks and guilt do not help. Twenty minutes on a Tuesday is worth more than a perfect plan you abandon in March. The progress bar counts levels, not days.

Before you start

Do I need to know kana first?

Levels 1–4 teach the sounds and introduce hiragana. You can start here with zero Japanese. If the characters still feel like decoration, spend a week with the kana charts in parallel — not instead.

Is this a replacement for a textbook or a teacher?

No. It is a structured spine you can follow on your own, with enough explanation to keep moving. A teacher is still the fastest way to fix pronunciation and to practise being misunderstood. Use the levels to arrive at class with better questions.

Why is Kansai-ben under every line?

Because the site is built around life in Japan, and standard Japanese plus a local colour line is a realistic combination. Standard is the portable base. Kansai-ben is the version you will hear in Osaka, Kyoto and Kobe — and a reminder that Japanese is a family of voices, not one recording. Where the Osaka word is exactly the same word, most of the kana vocabulary in levels 1–7, you will see only the standard line: there is nothing to add.

What happens after level 100?

You keep going with real material: newspaper columns, novels, workplace email, podcasts, and conversations that do not wait for you. The last levels teach you how to study from those sources, not how to stop needing them.
